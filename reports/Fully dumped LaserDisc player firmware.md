# One font read separates VP415 from completion

**No LaserDisc player can yet be recreated on a MiSTer purely from original, publicly dumped code and silicon data.** Every candidate fails on at least one undumped part, almost always the OSD character generator. The closest is the **Philips VP415** (PAL, the BBC Domesday LV-ROM player). Every MCU program on its control, drive and LV-ROM paths is publicly dumped with hashes: two 8031 EPROMs, the Z80 LV-DOS pair, two lookup-table EPROMs and the NEC 8041 mask ROM. Its only missing ROM-class item is the **Fujitsu MB88303's 448 x 5-bit on-die font**. That font has so far been transcribed from a 1986 datasheet figure, not read from a chip. The same catalogue MB88303 also sits in the **Sony LDP-2000**, so a font read from any real MB88303 would serve both players. The user's own **LD-V4300D** is blocked outright. Its mode-control MCU **PD0110A** (mask ROM) is not known to appear in any other player, and its **MB90061-101A** font is undumped. The one sharing route, the MB90061-101 in the **LD-V8000**, supplies a font donor but cannot stand in for PD0110A's program. Nobody is on record as having decapped any LaserDisc player chip. The gaps are closable, though: one MB88303 read (by decap or by logic-analysing its digital pixel pin) would bring the VP415 to "all programs and font original". Several older Pioneer players need only one custom display chip each.

## No player clears the bar today; the VP415 misses by one chip

The ranking below orders candidates by how little stands between them and a build that is original in every part. PAL is weighted up, and so is the cost of closing each gap. "Dumped" means publicly available with a hash. The confidence column covers the inventory and dump status, not how hard the gap is to close.

The ranking turns on a structural fact. **On every player with a schematic, the OSD font lives inside a mask-programmed or custom chip, never in a program EPROM.** A scan of all eleven VP415 images at strides 5-16 found no font table ([vp415-roms character-set](https://github.com/Domesday86/vp415-roms/blob/HEAD/character-set/README.md)). The LD-V8000's display generator sits on a serial bus separate from its EPROMs ([LD-V8000 SM](https://www.dragons-lair-project.com/tech/manuals/Pioneer_LD-V8000_Service_Manual.pdf)). Program dumps therefore come cheaply from socketed EPROMs and from MCS-48 parts that can be read electrically, while fonts come from nowhere. The VP415 wins because its OSD chip is a Fujitsu catalogue part with a published datasheet, the only OSD in the field with a documented specification. The Pioneer players lose to Pioneer custom display chips with no documentation at all.

## Philips VP415: every program dumped, the font only transcribed

The VP415 has three processor boards. **Module R (drive)** has an 8031 at 12 MHz with a 16 KB TMS27128 EPROM (IC7204), an MB88303 OSD (IC7212) fed from an 8155 port, and a "dedicated" Manchester-code reader, IC7211. **Module S (control)** has an 8031 at 11.059 MHz with a 64 KB TMS27512 (IC7202), plus an NEC D8041AHC-152 mask-ROM UPI handling serial and RC-5 input. **Module W (LV-ROM/SCSI)** has a Z80 running two LV-DOS EPROMs, two more EPROMs used as logic tables (SYNC, DESCR), and the same 8041 mask ([module R](https://github.com/Domesday86/vp415-roms/blob/HEAD/docs/module-r-drive.md), [module S](https://github.com/Domesday86/vp415-roms/blob/HEAD/docs/module-s-control.md), [firmware.md](https://github.com/Domesday86/vp415-service-guide/blob/HEAD/docs/reference/firmware.md)). Module X's LV-ROM decoder (SAA7000/7010/7020) and the SAA1043 sync generator are fixed-function silicon. Module U passes disc teletext through an SAA5230/5231 slicer without rendering it, so **no teletext font ROM is involved** ([module U](https://github.com/Domesday86/vp415-service-guide/blob/HEAD/docs/circuit-description/modules/u-analog-io.md)).

The dumps are curated with SHA-256 in Domesday86's `vp415-roms` repository and mirrored as zips on [domesday86.com](https://www.domesday86.com/?page_id=316) ([original-images README](https://github.com/Domesday86/vp415-roms/blob/HEAD/original-images/README.md)). Most also appear in MAME's `vp415` set with CRC32/SHA1 ([MAME vp415.cpp](https://github.com/mamedev/mame/blob/master/src/mame/skeleton/vp415.cpp)).

Three caveats stop this from being a clean "done". First, Philips warns against mixing LV-DOS 1.3 and 1.4. MAME's comments label 6805.3 "Version 1.3" and 6806.3 "Version 1.4", while Domesday86 dates both .3 programs to rev 1.4. By program number, MAME's pair is therefore a matched 1.4 set, but nobody has cross-matched MAME's SHA1 against Domesday86's SHA-256 ([vp415-roms README](https://github.com/Domesday86/vp415-roms/blob/HEAD/original-images/README.md)). Second, the claim that one 8041 image covers both the S and W positions "rests on the markings of one pair of boards from the same lot" (same source). Third, the printed checksum `BF90` for LVDOS#1 6805.3 disagrees with the dump's `0x8F90`. That is probably a typewriter B/8 slip, but it "cannot be ruled out without a second dump" (same source). The earlier DRIVE revisions (6803.4/.5) and CONTROL revisions (6804.4-.7) remain undumped. That does not block a single-revision build.

**The MB88303 font is the decisive gap, and it is a decision for you rather than a solved item.** The MB88303 is a Fujitsu NMOS TV Display Controller: 20 x 9 characters, 64 glyphs, a **448 x 5-bit character ROM on the die** ([character-set README](https://github.com/Domesday86/vp415-roms/blob/HEAD/character-set/README.md)). Domesday86 transcribed the glyphs from Figure 3(a) of the Edition 2.0 (October 1986) datasheet as `mb88303-font.bin`, 448 bytes, SHA-256 `c6595809d67a5174f48f1a55c7561324ee21f701325875cc04c6c7e6a7476b33`. They cross-checked it against mirror symmetry, against Figure 3(b), and against every string the DRIVE firmware displays, and fixed one stray dot in "2". Their own verdict is that it is "a transcription of a 1986 scan, so it is evidence rather than a dump", and that "what would settle it beyond doubt is reading a real MB88303" (same source). Glyphs the firmware never shows are confirmed only by the scan. MAME's `mb88303.cpp` carries a separate hand-entered `s_character_data[0x40][7]` table with no ROM region and no stated provenance ([MAME mb88303.cpp](https://github.com/mamedev/mame/blob/master/src/devices/video/mb88303.cpp)). **Under your rules neither counts as a chip dump.** Accepting the datasheet transcription would be your call to make explicitly.

The second open item is **IC7211 on module R**. The Philips circuit description gives no part number for it ([module R circuit description](https://github.com/Domesday86/vp415-service-guide/blob/HEAD/docs/circuit-description/modules/r-drive-processor.md)), and the firmware analysis treats its bit layout as a black box. The collective parts list holds six unexplained house numbers: PCF0706P/008, **PD0011**, PA0017, CLX1004, CLX5001 and GSCXB38113P01 ([electrical-parts.md](https://github.com/Domesday86/vp415-service-guide/blob/HEAD/docs/parts/electrical-parts.md)). The earlier research flagged PCF0706P/008 as a possible mask variant. A stronger inference follows from cross-reading the Pioneer notes. In the LD-V1000, **PD0011 is the "DECODER IC" that reads the Philips code on lines 16-18** ([LD-V1000 SM](https://www.dragons-lair-project.com/tech/manuals/Pioneer_LD-V1000_Service_Manual.pdf)). IC7211 is likewise a device that "reads Manchester codes from the clipped video signal". IC7211 is therefore plausibly a PD0011. Reading the marking from the CS 6 883 schematic or a board photo would settle it. If it is fixed logic, it needs recreating as logic; if it is a mask MCU, it needs dumping.

Two further boundaries apply to any VP415 build. MAME's driver fails self-test 073 ("a/d converted mirror pos. min."). The failure sits at the analog servo boundary, where MAME fakes the radial-mirror input with a DAC-window check ([MAME vp415.cpp](https://github.com/mamedev/mame/blob/master/src/mame/skeleton/vp415.cpp)). Recreating the deck, motors and mirror feedback is unavoidable "behaviour outside the firmware". The fixed-function Philips chips (SAA1043, SAA70xx) would also have to be recreated as logic from their datasheets. Neither is a ROM-dump question, but both raise the same faithfulness question that your project already faces with the LD-V4300D's mechanics.

The **VP410** is the VP415 without the Domesday SCSI/LV-ROM sandwich ([domesday86 VP410](https://www.domesday86.com/?page_id=1804)). Its CONTROL A EPROM is dumped: 64 KB, SHA-256 `9dee7647ab7480a4ae5fdfdf382001a2cd04413182835b5e0244c65693de9b40`. So is its own, *different*, 8041: SHA-256 `b061c815822c0e35282a8182a3d972ff8f67961bdd5522eefbfc938d62c9e512`, sum16 0xC014 against the VP415's 0xFC62 ([vp415-roms README](https://github.com/Domesday86/vp415-roms/blob/HEAD/original-images/README.md)). Its module R DRIVE is not separately dumped. Philips issued one combined VP410/415 release survey (CS 8 284), so DRIVE 6803.6 is probably shared, but that is unverified. A VP410 build is therefore only original if that survey or a VP410 board confirms the shared DRIVE, and it still needs the MB88303 font.

The LD-V4300D/4400 hashes were computed locally on the project's copies (`ref/PROVENANCE.md`). The Dragon's Lair Project also hosts a stock LD-V1000 image and a Konami Badlands variant, without hashes ([DLP Pioneer](https://www.dragons-lair-project.com/tech/ldguide/pioneer.asp)). Nobody has checked whether the stock image matches MAME's. **No Pioneer OSD font and no Pioneer mode-control or sub-CPU mask ROM is public.**

The **LD-V1000** (c.1983) runs a Z80A with an 8 KB 2764 EPROM. It pairs Z9 **PD0011** ("DECODER IC") with Z10 **PD0010** ("DISPLAY IC") ([LD-V1000 SM](https://www.dragons-lair-project.com/tech/manuals/Pioneer_LD-V1000_Service_Manual.pdf)). MAME runs the real EPROM on a Z80 core but models both customs behaviourally, and "add OSD" is still on its to-do list ([MAME ldv1000.cpp](https://github.com/mamedev/mame/blob/master/src/devices/machine/ldv1000.cpp)). The **PR-8210** (c.1982) is similar. Its 8049 mask ROM (UD6005A) is dumped, but the 42-pin **UD4001** "data processor" decodes the Philips code, stores data and performs "character display control" ([PR-8210-A SM](https://www.dragons-lair-project.com/tech/manuals/Pioneer_PR-8210-A_Service_Manual.pdf)). MAME replaces the UD4001 with a "mystery Pioneer PIA" and a hand-made glyph table in which most glyphs are `{ 0 }` ([MAME ldpr8210.cpp](https://github.com/mamedev/mame/blob/master/src/devices/machine/ldpr8210.cpp)). Each player is one custom chip from complete, but neither chip has a datasheet. Even with a captured font, their timing and attribute logic would still have to be reconstructed unless the die itself is imaged.

The **PR-7820** (c.1979) is the only Pioneer player whose control/OSD path is built entirely from catalogue parts. It has an F8 CPU set (MK3850N, MK3853N and MK90005N), a program in **two socketed 2716 EPROMs**, and a **National DM8678CABN** character generator fed from display RAM ([PR-7820 SM](https://www.dragons-lair-project.com/tech/manuals/Pioneer_PR-7820_Service_Manual.pdf)). Nothing is dumped, and MAME has only a stub ([MAME ldstub.cpp](https://github.com/mamedev/mame/blob/master/src/devices/machine/ldstub.cpp)). Any owner with an EPROM reader could dump the program. The DM8678's standard font should appear in National's 1980 memory databook ([archive.org](https://archive.org/download/1980NationalMemoryDataBook/1980_National_Memory_Data_Book.pdf)), but that has not been checked. Using that table would be another datasheet transcription and needs your ruling. Because the DM8678 is a catalogue ROM-based generator, a real chip may also be readable electrically by stepping its inputs. That is an inference, not a tested method. One identity check remains. If MK90005N is a 3851-class PSU rather than the 3861-class PIO the text implies, it holds mask ROM and becomes a fourth undumped device.

The **LD-V8000** (c.1990, NTSC) is the most important Pioneer for your project, because it shares the **MB90061-101** display generator with the LD-V4300D's MB90061-101A. Its main program runs on a ROMless NEC V25 (µPD70320) from two socketed EPROMs, **DYW1041 and DYW1042**. A later upgrade kit, DYW-1208/DYW-1244, is described in Pioneer bulletin #151402 ([PIB #151402](https://manuals.lddb.com/LD_Players/Pioneer/LD/LD-V8000/Documents/PIB%20%23151402%20-%20LD-V8000%20Version%20Upgrade.pdf)). Its board also carries IC6 MB90061-101 ("DISPLAY GENERATOR", serial ESYNC/SCLK/SI/CS/BUSY), IC8 PD0011A ("DECODER"), IC2 PD2021 ("DIGITAL SERVO"), IC5 PD4183 ("MULTI TRACK COUNTER") and IC14 **PD5109** ("SUB CPU", which reads the keys, drives the LEDs and receives the remote; its pin names look Mitsubishi) ([LD-V8000 SM](https://www.dragons-lair-project.com/tech/manuals/Pioneer_LD-V8000_Service_Manual.pdf)). None of it is dumped. A full LD-V8000 build needs the two EPROMs (easy), PD5109 (mask, decap-class), the MB90061-101 font and logic, and an answer on whether PD2021/PD4183 hold programs.

The **LD-V4300D** is blocked, with high confidence. Its IC1 PD0081A1 mechanism MCU runs the dumped external EPROM (DYW1362; the parts list names DYW1134, probably an earlier revision). Its IC2 **PD0110A** "MODE CONT. MCU" owns RS-232, keys, remote, the EEPROM, the PAL/NTSC switch and the serial feed to IC601, the **MB90061-101A** "Charactor generator". PD0110A has an internal mask ROM and no external bus ([LD-V4300D SM, DD86 copy](https://www.domesday86.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Pioneer-LD-V4300D-Service-Manual-DD86_2.pdf)). Parts sellers list PD0110A only as "IC,(CMOS),UCPU", and its core is unidentified ([instrumentalparts](https://instrumentalparts.com/ic-cmos-ucpu-pd0110a/)). No other model is known to contain it. The LD-V8000's EPROM program implements a related command set (the LD-V4400 protocol "is compatible with the Pioneer LD-V4200 and LD-V8000", per the [LaserDisc UK archive](https://laserdiscarchive.lddb.com/laserdisc_archive/pioneer/pioneer_ld-v4400/pioneer_ld-v4400.htm)). But it was written for a V25 driving LD-V8000 hardware. Running it in place of PD0110A would be a new, synthetic architecture, which your rules forbid. **The LD-V4300D is reachable only by dumping PD0110A itself**, plus an MB90061-101(A) font read. The service-mode "Mode PD0110A OK" self-checksum gives a ready-made validation target for a decap.

Pioneer's 1990s consumer players are worse targets. The CLD-S201 and CLD-D790 families pair a mask-ROM "MECHANISM CONTROL" MCU (PD0133B, PD0240B2) with a mask-ROM "MODE CONTROL" MCU (PD3213A, PDG178A). They have no EPROM, and the OSD is built into the DVP ([manualslib CLD-D790](https://www.manualslib.com/manual/4171715/Pioneer-Cld-D790.html), [CLD-S201](https://www.manualslib.com/manual/4258758/Pioneer-Cld-S201.html)). Nothing from them is dumped.

## Shared silicon turns three chips into multi-player keys

The hybrid rule makes a single real read of a shared chip count several times over. The table lists every cross-model match in the notes. Rows marked as inference rest on matching part numbers in OCR'd parts lists, not on proof that the silicon is the same.

| Chip | Players | Function | Status | What one read unlocks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fujitsu MB88303** (catalogue) | Philips VP415 (IC7212); VP410 (likely); Sony LDP-2000 | OSD, 64 x 5x7 on-die font | Datasheet transcription and a MAME hand table only | The VP415's last ROM-class gap; the LDP-2000's OSD |
| **Fujitsu MB90061-101 / -101A** | Pioneer LD-V8000 (IC6); LD-V4300D (IC601) | OSD display generator | Undumped; no datasheet found | The font for both, if -101 and -101A share a mask |
| **PD0011 / PD0011A** | LD-V1000 (Z9, decoder); LD-V8000 (IC8, decoder); VP415 parts list (unplaced); Sony LDP-1500P (OCR, beside "DATA DECODER") | Philips-code (Manchester) VBI decoder | Undumped; likely fixed logic (inference) | The decoder for up to four players, possibly including the VP415's IC7211 (inference) |
| **PD0010** | LD-V1000 (Z10, display IC); Sony LDP-1500P (OCR) | OSD/display | Undumped | The LD-V1000 OSD; possibly the LDP-1500P (PAL) character generator (inference) |
| **MB88201-173N, MB88201H-523M** | Sony LDP-2000; Sony LDP-1450 | 4-bit mask MCUs (512 B ROM) | Undumped | Both Sony players; MAME has the core ([mb88xx.cpp](https://github.com/mamedev/mame/blob/master/src/devices/cpu/mb88xx/mb88xx.cpp)) |
| **NEC D8041AHC-152** | VP415 modules S and W | UPI slave | Dumped | Already done; the VP410 uses a different image |
| **UD6005A (8049)** | PR-8210; PR-8210-A | Main CPU | Dumped | Already done |

The MB88303 row matters most because it closes a PAL player. The MB88303 is a Fujitsu catalogue part, so its font is fixed by the die. A read from *any* MB88303, whether from a VP415, VP410, LDP-2000 or another product, is an original-silicon font for the VP415 ([character-set README](https://github.com/Domesday86/vp415-roms/blob/HEAD/character-set/README.md); [LDP-2000 SM](https://archive.org/details/sony-ldp-2000-service-manual)). That holds only if Fujitsu never shipped MB88303 font variants. Neither the Philips stock code (4822 209 71278) nor the Sony listing shows a mask suffix, but this is unconfirmed. The LDP-2000 itself is a poor build target: nothing in it is dumped, it is NTSC-only, and it also needs the two MB88201 masks. As a donor of a sacrificial MB88303, though, it is as good as a VP415.

The PD0010/PD0011 row is the notes' most surprising cross-link. "PD" plus four digits is Pioneer's house-number style (PD0110A, PD0081A1, PD5109, PD2021). Finding PD0011 in a Philips parts list and PD0010/PD0011 in a Sony PAL player's OCR ([LDP-1500P SM](https://archive.org/details/ldp1500p)) suggests a Philips-code decoder and display chipset sold across manufacturers. If confirmed from schematic images, characterising PD0010 would serve the LD-V1000 and possibly the PAL LDP-1500P, and PD0011 would serve every player in the row. Neither part has a datasheet, so both would need die imaging to avoid reconstructed logic.

Three hybrid combinations are worth naming. The **VP415 plus a real MB88303 font from any donor** is the only combination that reaches "every ROM and font original" with one chip read, and it is PAL. The **LD-V4300D plus an LD-V8000-sourced MB90061-101 font** covers the font gap, but PD0110A still blocks it. The **LD-V1000 plus a PD0010/PD0011 read** completes an NTSC player that MAME already runs at Z80 level. No combination gives the LD-V4300D a donor program for PD0110A.

## Sony, Hitachi, Panasonic and consumer players offer nothing dumped

**No Sony, Hitachi or Panasonic player has any public program or font dump.** MAME marks `ldp1000_bios.bin` and `ldp1450_bios.bin` as `NO_DUMP` and lists the LDP-1450 text overlay as a to-do ([ldp1000.cpp](https://github.com/mamedev/mame/blob/master/src/devices/machine/ldp1000.cpp), [ldp1450.cpp](https://github.com/mamedev/mame/blob/master/src/devices/machine/ldp1450.cpp)). The `ldp1450hle` font is a hand-coded 16 x 16 table flagged as "needs aligning with real hardware" ([ldp1450hle.cpp](https://github.com/mamedev/mame/blob/master/src/devices/machine/ldp1450hle.cpp)). Hypseus-Singe's LDP-1000/1450 and VIP-9500SG code emulates the command protocol only ([Hypseus-Singe ldp-in](https://github.com/DirtBagXon/hypseus-singe/tree/master/src/ldp-in)).

The service manuals do show which Sony players would be cheapest to dump. The **LDP-2000** keeps its system program in a **socketed 2732 EPROM on an HD68P01V07 piggyback 6801**, beside the two MB88201 masks and the MB88303 ([LDP-2000 SM](https://archive.org/details/sony-ldp-2000-service-manual)). The **LDP-1450** uses an HD637A01Y0P (probably a 16 KB OTP 63701Y0 that a programmer can read, medium confidence) plus the same MB88201 pair and an MB89010A-109A in the character-generator area. MAME's 8 KB BIOS placeholder size is likely wrong ([LDP-1450 SM](https://archive.org/details/manual_LDP1450_SM_SONY_EN)). The **LDP-1500P** is the best-documented PAL Sony, but its HD63A01Y0 system controller is mask ROM (code A86), and its character-generator IC is unidentified in the OCR ([LDP-1500P SM](https://archive.org/details/ldp1500p)). The PAL **MDP-650D** consumer combi is all mask: MB89795-DCX615, CXP50116-417Q and an M50455-196FP OSD. MAME's M50458 font cannot substitute for the M50455 ([MDP-450/650D SM](https://archive.org/details/sony-mdp450-mdp650d-service-manual), [MAME m50458.cpp](https://github.com/mamedev/mame/blob/master/src/devices/video/m50458.cpp)). Panasonic's LX-1000U uses MN188xx mask MCUs ([LX-1000U SM](https://archive.org/details/lx-1000-u_202407)). No chip list was found for any Hitachi, Toshiba, Mitsubishi, Thomson or OPA player. The unread Sony LDP-1550P manual on the DLP is the most important unread document for a PAL Sony target ([DLP Sony](https://www.dragons-lair-project.com/tech/ldguide/sony.asp)).

## Nobody has decapped a LaserDisc chip; four routes could close the gaps

**No decap or mask-ROM extraction of any LaserDisc player chip is on public record.** That covers PD0110A, PD0081A1, PD5109, UD4001, PD0010/0011, MB90061, MB88303, D8041AHC and IC7211. Guru's decap list (169 chips) holds no Pioneer or LaserDisc parts, and CAPS0ff has no LaserDisc, MELPS 740 or Fujitsu MB88/MB90 posts ([Guru Decapping Project](https://gurudumps.otenko.com/decap/), [CAPS0ff](https://caps0ff.blogspot.com/)). Every player MCU dump that exists came from an electrically readable part: MCS-48 internal ROM or EPROM, or an external EPROM.

The first route needs no special equipment: **read the socketed EPROMs.** The LD-V8000's DYW1041/1042 (or 1208/1244), the PR-7820's two 2716s and the LDP-2000's 2732 need only an owner and a standard programmer. These reads complete no player on their own, but they turn the LD-V8000 and PR-7820 from "nothing dumped" into "one or two chips short". MCS-48 parts can be tried in verify mode, which is presumably how the UD6005A was read, though that is unconfirmed.

The second route is the **digital pixel-pin capture**, and it fits the MB88303 well. Drive a real MB88303 with all 64 codes, using the real DRIVE firmware or a bench rig, and log its VOW output pin with a logic analyser. VOW is the raw pixel stream before it is mixed into the composite video, so this reads what the silicon itself emits rather than a lossy video capture. The chip's inter-dot filling must be accounted for ([character-set README](https://github.com/Domesday86/vp415-roms/blob/HEAD/character-set/README.md)). Whether a capture of a chip's own digital output counts as a "dump" is **a decision for you**. It is original silicon data, not a transcription, but it is not a ROM read either. The same approach could recover the MB90061-101(A) font by first sniffing the PD0110A-to-IC601 serial bus (DI/DO/SCK1/DBUSY) on a working LD-V4300D. That would give the font but not the chip's internal logic, which has no datasheet.

The third route is **decap and optical ROM reading**, which is required for PD0110A, PD5109 and the custom Pioneer display chips, and is the most authoritative option for any font. No documented non-destructive readout exists for MELPS 740-class mask parts. CNVss only selects internal or external boot, and MAME still lists several M50747 arcade MCUs as undumped ([MAME undumped list](https://mamedev.emulab.it/undumped/index.php?title=Bad_dump)). Optical extraction is established practice. Travis Goodspeed's [MaskROMTool](https://github.com/travisgoodspeed/maskromtool) and John McMaster's imaging toolchain ([hardwear.io](https://hardwear.io/virtual-con-2020/speakers/capturing-mask-roms.php)) are examples, and a font ROM is easier to verify than program ROM because glyphs are self-evident. Identifying PD0110A's core from its pinout should come first, because it decides how to descramble the ROM.

The fourth route is **services and sacrificial chips**. CAPS0ff put itself on hold in January 2024. Guru still accepts loose or donated chips, and his last published lab benchmark was **US$200 per chip**. He no longer scraps working boards, so a requester must supply the chip or buy the board ([Guru](https://gurudumps.otenko.com/decap/), [CAPS0ff](https://caps0ff.blogspot.com/)). John McMaster works on "microcontroller data extraction using lasers, microscopes, and power analysis" and can be approached directly ([hardwear.io](https://hardwear.io/netherlands-2020/speakers/john-mcmaster.php)). Decap is destructive and first attempts fail, so budget for at least two chips per part. PD0110A appears in a parts seller's catalogue ([instrumentalparts](https://instrumentalparts.com/ic-cmos-ucpu-pd0110a/)), and dead LD-V4300D, LD-V4400 and LD-V8000 boards are the other source. Stock levels and current decap prices were not confirmed.

## Conclusion

The notes change the shape of the problem. "Fully dumped" is not limited by how hard program ROMs are to dump. Those are mostly done or are socketed EPROMs waiting for an owner. The limit is **OSD silicon**, and it is specific: one catalogue Fujitsu chip (MB88303) stands between a PAL player and completion, while undocumented customs (MB90061, PD0010, UD4001) gate the Pioneer line. The most efficient next move is therefore a single MB88303 read, by VOW capture or decap, from whichever donor is cheapest: VP415, VP410 or Sony LDP-2000. That one artefact would let a VP415 recreation run entirely on original code and font. It would also give the community its first LaserDisc OSD font read from silicon.

For the LD-V4300D, the honest status is that nothing short of decapping PD0110A will do. The LD-V8000 helps with the font but cannot substitute for the program. If you want to pursue it, buy at least two PD0110A chips, identify the core from the pinout, and commission an optical read, with the "Mode PD0110A OK" checksum as proof of a correct dump. Meanwhile, rulings 1 to 4 in the table above set the bar every other candidate is measured against, and they are worth deciding before any money is spent.
